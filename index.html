Add a tone from the carrier's harmonic series

How the ratios work. Any vibrating string or column of air sounds its base frequency plus whole-number multiples of it: 2×, 3×, 4×, 5× and so on. This is the harmonic series, and musical intervals come straight from it. Harmonics 1 → 2 make an octave (2 : 1), 2 → 3 a perfect fifth (3 : 2), 3 → 4 a perfect fourth (4 : 3), 4 → 5 a major third (5 : 4). Tones in simple ratios with the carrier blend smoothly. Tones that are slightly off a simple ratio create a slow wobble of their own.

Pulse options. "Every 2nd beat" switches the tone on at half the beat rate, "every 3rd" at a third, and so on. Over a 6 Hz theta beat, a tone pulsing every 2nd beat gives 3 pulses a second, a 2-against-1 rhythm. Pulsed layers can drift slightly out of step with a binaural beat because the brain creates that beat, not the speaker.

Breathing pacer

Slow breathing near 6 breaths a minute is the best-studied way to raise vagal tone, measured as heart-rate variability.9 A longer exhale than inhale also leans toward the parasympathetic side, because the heart slows a little on every exhale.

Background & sources

Everything above is the tool. Everything below explains where the bands, numbers and claims come from, with links to the research.

Where the band names came from

1924

Hans Berger, a German psychiatrist, records the first human EEG and publishes it in 1929.1 He sees a large, regular rhythm near 10 per second when people rest with eyes closed that disappears when they open their eyes or do mental arithmetic. He calls it alpha, and the smaller, faster waves that replace it beta. The letters simply mean first and second.

1936

W. Grey Walter, using EEG to locate brain tumours in Bristol, names the very slow waves under 4 Hz delta.2

1938

Herbert Jasper and H. L. Andrews use gamma for activity faster than beta.3

1944

Walter and Dovey name the 4–7 Hz rhythm theta, the letter chosen for the thalamus, where they thought it came from.4

1968

Sleep staging is standardised (Rechtschaffen and Kales, now the AASM manual).5 Deep N3 sleep is defined by delta waves, drowsy N1 by theta, and relaxed eyes-closed wakefulness by posterior alpha.

So the links are observations. "Delta = deep sleep" means the sleeping brain produces delta waves. It does not by itself show that hearing a 2 Hz beat causes sleep. The step from one to the other is the entrainment hypothesis: that a rhythmic sound pulls brain rhythms toward its own rate. The brain does follow some rhythmic sounds, most clearly near 40 Hz. Whether binaural beats shift the EEG is unsettled. A 2023 systematic review found 5 of 14 studies supported it, 8 did not and 1 was mixed.7 Band edges also vary a little between textbooks (alpha 8–12 or 8–13 Hz).

How each link was established

  • Alpha → calm, eyes closed. Berger saw alpha appear when people relaxed with their eyes closed and disappear when they opened their eyes or did mental arithmetic ("alpha blocking"). Modern studies still use this test. Barry and colleagues recorded 28 adults resting with eyes closed and then open. Alpha fell when eyes opened, and people with lower alpha had higher skin conductance, a measure of arousal.16 That inverse link between alpha and arousal is why alpha is called "relaxed wakefulness". Klimesch's review adds that alpha also rises and falls with memory tasks, so it is not only a relaxation signal.15
  • Theta → drowsiness and memory. Overnight sleep recordings showed theta taking over as alpha fades at sleep onset, which became stage N1.5 Theta also rises while people learn new information,15 and in animals hippocampal theta tracks navigation and memory.17 The meditation link comes from EEG studies of meditators.
  • Delta → deep sleep. Sleep EEG from the late 1930s onward showed large slow waves in the deepest, hardest-to-wake sleep. The AASM rules define N3 as 20% or more of a 30-second epoch filled with 0.5–2 Hz waves over 75 µV.5 In awake adults, delta mostly signals drowsiness or injury. Knyazev's review covers its links to homeostatic and motivational processes.18
  • Beta → alert, active thinking. Beta is the faster activity Berger saw replace alpha when eyes opened and people concentrated. It is strongest over the motor cortex and drops when you move. Engel and Fries argue it signals keeping the current motor or mental state going.19
  • Gamma → attention, and 40 Hz. In 1981 Galambos showed that clicks repeated about 40 times a second make the human brain produce a steady 40 Hz response. It disappears under surgical anesthesia.20 This "auditory steady-state response" is the clearest case of the brain actually following a sound's rhythm, and it underlies the 40 Hz Alzheimer's work.11,12

Binaural beats

Heinrich Dove described binaural beats in 1839. Gerald Oster's 1973 Scientific American article brought them into modern research,6 and Robert Monroe's Hemi-Sync recordings made them popular in the 1970s. A 2019 meta-analysis of 22 studies found a medium effect on anxiety, memory, attention and pain (g = 0.45).8 The EEG evidence for why it works is much weaker, as above.

Where the preset numbers come from

  • 40 Hz gamma. MIT studies flickered light and played sound at 40 Hz to mice that model Alzheimer's disease, and reduced amyloid and improved memory.11,12 Human trials are ongoing.
  • 440 vs 432 Hz. A = 440 Hz is the international concert-pitch standard (agreed in 1939, ISO 16 since 1975). 432 Hz is a popular alternative tuning. No study has shown a health difference.
  • Solfeggio tones (174–963 Hz). These come from 1970s–1990s writings (Joseph Puleo, then Leonard Horowitz) that derived them by numerology from Bible verse numbers. Medieval solfège named notes but had no fixed pitch in hertz. Claims such as "528 Hz repairs DNA" are unsupported.
  • 136.1 Hz Om. Hans Cousto's "cosmic octave" took the length of Earth's year and doubled its frequency 32 times to land in the audible range.
  • 7.83 Hz Schumann. This is the lowest electromagnetic resonance of the gap between Earth's surface and the ionosphere, predicted by Winfried Schumann in 1952 and measured in 1960.13 Its overlap with the theta/alpha border is a coincidence often used in marketing.
  • Humming. Humming raises nasal nitric oxide about 15-fold.14 It also lengthens the exhale, which is likely most of its calming effect. That is why a low drone you can hum along with is a useful extra tone.

Before you use it

Binaural needs headphones. Each ear has to get its own tone. Through speakers the two mix in the air and become a monaural beat. Isochronic and monaural beats work on speakers.

Keep it quiet and safe. Start low, since these tones can get loud in headphones. Don't listen while driving. People with seizure disorders should skip rhythmic pulsing sounds or check with their doctor first. Tones do not stimulate the vagus nerve directly. Slow breathing does the measurable work.10

On iPhone, turn off silent mode or the browser plays nothing. Full screen on iPhone opens as a full-window view.
